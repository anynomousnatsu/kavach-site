What the Food Hygiene and Quality Act 2081 requires from your restaurant

Nepal replaced its 1966 Food Act with the Food Hygiene and Quality Act 2081 (2024). If you run a restaurant, café, bakery or hotel kitchen, the practical change is simple: pest control moved from "good practice" to a licensing requirement — and inspectors can ask you to show it.

The short version

Every food business in Nepal — restaurants, cafés, bakeries, hotels, cold stores, importers — requires a DFTQC licence, now processed through the NeFFILS digital platform. DFTQC's premises requirements explicitly include pest-control measures, alongside the adoption of GMP/GHP and HACCP-aligned hygiene practice. Inspections look at kitchen hygiene, water and waste management — and pest evidence sits inside all three.

Here is the sentence that matters most: the law requires you to have pest control in place, and an inspection requires you to be able to show something. A verbal "we have a guy who sprays" is not something you can show.

What an inspector actually looks at

Inspection practice varies, but the pattern in Kathmandu Valley is consistent. Expect attention on:

  1. 1

    Evidence of pests. Droppings in drawers and along skirtings, gnawed packaging, live insects in the dry store, fly activity in the dining room. This is the part you cannot argue with — it is either there or it isn't.

  2. 2

    Structural risk. Gaps under back doors, uncovered drains, broken screens, cardboard accumulation. These are findings even with no pest in sight.

  3. 3

    Your pest-control arrangement. Who does it, how often, and with what. This is where a written record changes the conversation entirely — and where most Kathmandu restaurants have nothing.

  4. 4

    Chemical safety. What is being applied near food, and whether it's a registered product. An unlabeled bottle of "medicine" under the sink is a worse finding than a cockroach.

The evidence file that ends the conversation

In markets where food-safety enforcement matured earlier (India's FSSAI regime, HACCP audits everywhere), a standard evidence package emerged. It is exactly what we build for every client:

  • A

    A service logbook on-site. Every visit: date, technician, areas treated, products with batch numbers, activity observed, corrective actions. An inspector who opens a maintained logbook usually closes it satisfied.

  • B

    Product traceability. Batch numbers connecting what was applied in your kitchen to a licensed importer's invoice. This also protects you if a chemical question ever arises.

  • C

    Monitoring records. Numbered stations and monthly readings showing pest pressure trending down — proof of a programme, not a panic response.

  • D

    A current certificate. Visible proof of an active arrangement, with dates and a phone number the inspector can call.

See a complete interactive sample on The Record — visit log, station map, batch table, trend chart and certificate.

"But nobody is checking yet"

Partly true, and worth being honest about. Enforcement capacity is building, and inspection frequency today is uneven. Three reasons not to wait:

First, licensing is the checkpoint. New licences and renewals flow through NeFFILS, and premises requirements are assessed there — you will meet this standard eventually, on someone else's schedule if not yours.

Second, other people's auditors are already asking. Delivery platforms, corporate catering clients, and foreign tour operators increasingly request hygiene evidence from venues. The DFTQC file and their checklist are substantially the same document.

Third, the pests don't care about enforcement schedules. The file is a by-product of a programme that keeps cockroaches out of your kitchen. The compliance value is the bonus, not the point.

Ready in one week — a checklist

  1. 1

    Walk your own kitchen with a torch tonight: drawer runners, under the cold room compressor, drain covers, the gap under the back door. (Or use our risk map.)

  2. 2

    Fix the free things: cardboard out same-day, food in sealed bins overnight, drains covered.

  3. 3

    Put a real arrangement in place — whoever you choose, insist on a logbook, batch numbers and a certificate. If your current provider can't offer that, that tells you something.

  4. 4

    Keep every pest-control invoice and bill in one folder with the logbook. That folder is your inspection answer.
